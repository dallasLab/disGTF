
Population dynamics are influenced by several factors that also determine where species exist in geographic space. The species climatic niche, the set of environmental conditions that allow a species to persist indefinitely in an area, is perhaps one of the most important such factors as species have low population growth rates and may be unable to occupy areas that have unsuitable environments \parencite{pulliam2000}. However, dispersal limitation might prevent species from occupying all environmentally suitable areas in the geographic space \parencite{soberon2007}, and dispersal may also allow the persistence of populations in unsuitable environments through a continuous influx of individuals \parencite{pulliam2000}. Species life-history traits and biotic interactions may further affect population dynamics and might influence where species occur \parencite{brown1996, paquette2021}, suggesting that a combination of different factors determine both species distributions and their population dynamics. 

Spatial differences in population dynamics are prevalent and understanding where species have smaller and more variable populations is of interest to applied and theoretical ecologists \parencite{lundberg2000, burgess2020}. For instance, if populations are smaller and more variable near the edges of species geographic ranges or climatic niches, then these populations may face a higher extinction risk, and conservation efforts might be directed to these vulnerable populations. Alternatively, decreasing population density and increasing population variability towards geographic range edges is an assumption made by some theoretical models that have assessed the origin and maintenance of stable geographic range limits \parencite{sexton2009, pironon2017}. Consequently, there has been an increased effort to gauge support for the existence of these geographic patterns of population dynamics across several species \parencite{sagarin2002, sagarin2006, pironon2017}.



A challenge when evaluating geographic patterns of population dynamics is that there is a lack of knowledge on the geographic distribution of several species \parencite{hortal2015}. This leads to an obvious problem where we are unable to know the geographic position of populations within species ranges if we have incomplete knowledge on species geographic distributions. This may restrict the number of species where the occurrence of geographic patterns of population dynamics can be examined, representing a limitation for the assessment of geographic patterns of population dynamics. Species distribution models (SDMs) are correlative approaches that relate species occurrence records with environmental conditions that are used to estimate species geographic ranges \parencite{elith2009, elith2010} that could be used to address this problem. Species distribution models produce habitat suitability estimates such that it has been suggested that higher habitat suitability values may indicate more favorable conditions for species where they would be able to achieve larger populations in such locations \parencite{weber2017, gomes2018}. This indicates that SDMs may not only be useful to estimate species geographic distributions, but also to estimate population densities across wide geographical extents. 

SDMs have become a key tool for answering of macroecological and biogeographic questions, and the ongoing development of several \textit{R} packages have simplified the use of SDMs. However, users are often faced with a wide range of methodological steps that need to be taken and that can directly affect the quality of their models. Thinning occurrence records (i.e., the removal of some occurrence points from the original occurrence dataset) is a methodological step commonly used to address biases that are often found in occurrence data that would improve SDMs performance \parencite{anderson2010,veloz2009}. Occurrence data can be thinned based on how close they are in the geographic or environmental space, such that only the most spatially or environmentally unique occurrences would be used in the modeling process \parencite{varela2014,aiello2015}. Despite thinning approaches being widely used in studies that make use of SDMs, assessments on the effectiveness of thinning approaches on SDMs are often of limited scope, leaving open questions about the utility of these approaches when modeling species distributions. 
 
In this dissertation, I combine observational data, simulations, and experimental approaches to answer questions related to the use of SDMs to estimate species geographic ranges and population densities and to examine the occurrence of patterns of population dynamics across geographic and climatic space. In Chapter 2 I evaluate the effects that thinning occurrence records have on the performance of SDMs. One of the challenges when thinning occurrence records is choosing the appropriate thinning distance \parencite{castellanos2019}, as larger distances may leave insufficient records to accurately model the species distributions. In this case, thinning might decrease model performance because SDMs tend to perform poorly when only a few number of occurrence records are used \parencite{gaul2020}.  The effects of thinning on SDMs performance were assessed considering different thinning distances for over 400 real and virtual species, four different modeling algorithms, and different types of spatial biases that occurrence records may have.

In Chapter 3 I examine how different factors influence the relationship between habitat suitability estimates produced by SDMs and population densities. Despite earlier studies indicating that habitat suitability relates to population density, more recent analyses considering a wide range of species rarely find support for the occurrence of this relationship \parencite{dallas2018, santini2019, lee2022}. It is often suggested that factors such as dispersal and resource availability are responsible for the weak, or absent, relationship between habitat suitability and population density \parencite{weber2017}. However, the effects of such factors on the relationship between habitat suitability and population density are often not explicitly considered in such studies. To fill this gap, I simulated the population dynamics of 200 species that differed in their responses to environmental conditions to understand how demographic stochasticity, dispersal, and intraspecific competition affects how habitat suitability relates to population density. 

In Chapter 4 I review the prevalence of patterns of population variability across geographic ranges. Populations are often assumed to smaller and more variable near geographic range edges because of less suitable environmental conditions in these areas \parencite{brown1984, lawton1993}. While the occurrence of geographic patterns of population density has been widely debated over the past decades \parencite{sagarin2002, sagarin2006, pironon2017}, less attention has been paid to patterns of population variability. I show that there is limited evidence supporting the expectation that populations vary more near the edges of species geographic ranges. I propose that species performance curves, which describe the relationship between population growth rate and environmental conditions, can be used to disentangle geographic patterns of population variability. Nevertheless, empirical assessments of geographic patterns of population variability often consider five or less species, such that it remains an open question whether these relationships may become more or less prevalent when considering several species. In Chapter 5 I address this gap and I assess how the population variability of 97 resident North American birds relates to their geographic range position, climatic niche position, and to the amount of temperature variability that they experience.  

In Chapter 6 I perform a microcosm experiment to evaluate the effects of degree distribution (i.e., the number of connections that populations share in networks) on metapopulation size, variability, and persistence of the water flea \textit{Daphnia dentifera}. Simulation studies suggest that spatial networks where populations have high variability in their number of connections may persist for longer periods of time despite having smaller sizes than spatial networks where populations have similar number of connections \parencite{gilarranz2012}. This suggests that differences in the degree distribution of spatial networks alone can be enough to influence the dynamics that are observed in metapopulations, though empirical evidence supporting these findings is lacking. I considered three types of spatial networks that had the same number of nodes and links, but that differed in their degree distribution, to examine the effects of degree distribution on the metapopulation dynamics of real species.  

Collectively, this body of work aimed to assess how different factors that affect populations influence the distribution and predictability of population dynamics across broad geographic extents. I have used different methodological approaches and data on several species to accomplish these goals. As species experience widespread differences in their population dynamics \parencite{lundberg2000}, it becomes increasingly important to evaluate the predictability of these dynamics as this information can be potentially used to understand which populations may be more vulnerable to ongoing changes in environmental conditions.
